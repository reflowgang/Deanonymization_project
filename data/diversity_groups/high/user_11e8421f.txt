Yep, this is the right advice. Sometimes you find products that do put LEDs straight in parallel but these are the products that end up working crappy.
If you have them all running in parallel right now, it would probably be least work to give each of them their own resistor (clip a lead on each one and solder a resistor in). This results in the least rewiring work. But, better efficiency is attained by putting them in series groups and then less power is lost in the resistors compared to usefully used in the LEDs. A 2V LED with resistor to take it up to ~12-14V is under 20% efficient, but for low power LEDs probably isn't a huge problem. 600 ohm resistors should work to give them 20mA at 14V, but each resistor will burn about 1/4 of a watt. So you should probably use 1/3W or higher rated resistors (maybe this isn't very important if you are soldering them straight onto a heavier wire with a short lead on the resistor side - the wire will heatsink it).

If a clock transition isn't fast enough, you should consider buffering it with a schmitt trigger based logic chip or buffer. For example, I made a single pushbutton controlled two light switch with a dual D flip flop chip (state storing), quad schmitt trigger nand (switch debouncing, some power-on reset logic, doubled up inputs for inverters). Q' just went back to data, and either Q or Q' became the clock for the second flipflop. The most challenging part was making the power-on reset work under various conditions: power input slowly rising, quickly rising, partial power-off (voltage never totally disappearing), all these conditions with the debounce capacitor in different state of charge, etc. I ended up using the power LED as part of the power-on reset circuit (as a voltage reference of sorts)... I wish I would have made a schematic of all the weirdness. Anyway the constraint I worked with is that accidental output activation isn't allowed. It is okay if it loses state and turns both outputs off, but not turning something on that was off, through power glitches. Anyway, the long and short of it, is you can use an RC filter into a schmitt trigger logic chip and it does a pretty decent job of debouncing. It might not catch some really weird corner cases of very momentary partial contact (the enforced delay between presses might become rather short, but it's still there), but it did perfectly fine with the real switch I had connected to it. In retrospect, even something this simple is a job for a damn microcontroller. I just really wanted to do a project playing with some raw logic chips.
