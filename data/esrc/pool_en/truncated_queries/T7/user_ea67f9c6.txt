It's difficult to give advice like this, because I don't want to stomp out anything unique in your writing that might become something great later on, but I'll just give one example. Mind you, this is a very subjective sort of critique. &gt; Langden continued to tread down the road, until he came across a bridge. Immediately, you give us a character and sense of space. Awesome. &gt; He could only see because of the frequent lamp posts evenly spread on the path. A common alternative would be to imply that the reader is already knows we're viewing this scene from your protagonist's perspective. This makes "He could only see" a bit redundant, unless there is something specifically unique about your character's sense of sight. Often times once the reader is in your protagonist's point of view, you can just go on with descriptions and assume your reader will know who it's coming from. If you used, "Lanterns, evenly spaced, were the only road's source of light." We would know Langen is seeing this. He's the only character in the scene so far! An alternative is to move the pronoun to add superior mental imagery. Here's a couple lines from the intro of a Sanderson novel: "Wax peeked up over the knotted boards, scanning the empty town. **Blue lines hovered in his vision,** extending from his chest to point at nearby sources of metal, a result of his Allomancy." He doesn't say, "He could see blue lines..." Instead he turns the visuals into the subject. I don't think there's anything wrong about pronoun subject use, in fact Sanderson does that exact thing in The Alloy of Law's first paragraph, but the repetitious use of it can wear your reader out.
