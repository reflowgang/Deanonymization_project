In *theory* it might be possible, there does (supposedly) [exist malware which can persist between rebuilds](https://www.securityartwork.es/2013/10/30/badbios-2/?lang=en). If you can manage to store code between installs *and* somehow get it to execute once the machine is re-installed you could achieve permanent persistence. *However* the more likely vector for apparently persistent infections is backed up data and/or external media that was previously infected being re-used on a new install. So, while it may be theoretically possible, there's nothing out there that is *proven* to exist, and if it did occur on a non-targeted basis, the stars would have to massively align and you would likely require a very specific combination of hardware for it to work. And while you can have a nefarious piece of hardware resemble something else (i.e. the mentioned Defcon Mouse hack, USB Rubber Ducky and HID attacks in general), both physically and electronically, this is not the same as malware hiding inside a *generic* piece of hardware.

The idea of [SD-WAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SD-WAN) is that it actually *helps* you get around these issues. It's just a term for the bundling up of various automated techniques to separate the underlying WAN fabric from the control of traffic flow. MPLS is part of SD-WAN, as it decouples your traffic from the medium it flows under, essentially you don;t care if the traffic is routed over an internet bound VPN or the MPLS as long as it gets to its intended destination by the most efficient method. For your specific use-case SD-WAN technologies will allow traffic to route around individual tunnel outages. As they *do not* have an SD-WAN overlay, when this occurs, we have to manually configure VPN routing; so if site A in china is inaccessible to site B outside of china, but site C in china is accessible from site B, and site A is accessible to site C, then we have to re-route all traffic to site A from B via site C and all to site B from A via C. Ideally, SD-WAN would just take care of this and automatically reroute, and more importantly, put routes back if/when the tunnel comes back.
