&gt;all the Xi = the union of the Xi. This is meaningless. The Xi aren't sets; you cannot take the union of them. What we are taking the union of is *events*. It sounds like you are working off a superficial interpretation where you see the word 'all' and leap immediately to 'union', which is why you are confused. Intersections of events are conjunctions ("and"), and unions of events are disjunctions ("or"). These are simply the definition of intersection and union: "y∈A∩B" means "y∈A and y∈B". If we want Xi&gt;x for all i - that is, we want X1&gt;x and X2&gt;2 and X3&gt;x and so on - that's an intersection of events, because we want *all* of the events to happen. &gt;Then we have proof in S that there is no counter example to RH in S This is where your reasoning fails. In fact, the sentence above is meaningless! Axiomatic systems don't have counterexamples because axiomatic systems don't have objects at all. If you want to talk about an actual object, you need to look at a *model* of the axioms. There's more than one model of the naturals (this is essentially what the first incompleteness theorem shows), so there is no reason to expect to be able to use the same counterexample for all of them. When we say something is *undecidable*, that means it cannot be proved from the axioms. By the [completeness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del's_completeness_theorem), statements are provable iff they are true in every model of the axioms, so the undecidable statements are precisely those which are true in some models and false in others. For a concrete example, take your axiomatic system to be the group axioms. A model of those axioms is a group. So tell me: in a group G, is there a counterexample to the claim "for all a,b in G, ab=ba"? If you're at all familiar with group theory, you will immediately realize that this is a ridiculous question: it depends on *which* group I'm asking about. That sentence is undecidable from the group axioms, precisely because some groups are commutative and other groups aren't. But the natural numbers are a very special case, because they have a *standard* model: yeah, there are other models too, but what we're really interested in is the truth value *in the standard model*. Moreover, the standard model of the naturals embeds into every other model. RH can be reduced to a certain statement about the natural numbers, which roughly says that no natural number with certain properties exists. If you show that's undecidable, that means that *some* models contain counterexamples, but other models don't. If there was a counterexample in the standard model, it would be in every model - so in this case, you've proven that RH is true in the standard model. One obvious method would be: interpolate between the world records to get a function that says "at this bodyweight W, the best possible performance is F(W)". Then if you have strength 82 and skill 73, your lift is (82/100)x(73/100)xF(W). This makes your "strength score" and "skill score" have an obvious meaning: a score of X means you are X% as good as the best. Getting that interpolation function is potentially tricky, because you have to decide what kind of function to interpolate with, and there are infinitely many ways to do this. Polynomials are popular - the Wilks formula in powerlifting is based on polynomial interpolation - but you still have to decide what degree of polynomial to use. Now, here's the trick: you probably don't actually want to interpolate exactly through world records, since after all, records only represent the best that has been done so far, not the best possible. What you actually want is some kind of reasonably smooth curve that doesn't predict anything too far from actual records, not a horrible seventh-degree polynomial that hits them exactly but does crazy things in between. There are [good theoretical reasons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allometry#Allometric_scaling) to think that a power law is the right relationship between bodyweight and strength. Specifically, your function should be F(W)=kW^(4/3), where k is a constant. For more on this topic - in the context of powerlifting instead of weightlifting, but the same considerations apply - see [this article](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/whos-the-most-impressive-powerlifter/) by Greg Nuckols.

If you want to prove that |A|&lt;|B|, you need to show that there are injections from A to B, but no bijections from A to B. That is, given any function f:A to B, you need to show f is not a surjection. This, in turn, means you need to prove that there exists an element of B not in the image of f. Ostensibly this element should somehow depend on the values of f(a) for a in A. And if your argument is constructive - you consider the values of f(a), and use them to construct a b in B\f(A) - then it's probably going to be called a "diagonalization argument", because that's the word people use for such things. But depending on the proof, it may not *look* like Cantor's diagonal argument in any obvious way; even [Cantor's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor's_theorem) requires you to squint a bit before you can see the "diagonal" being used. Sometimes you can prove it *non*-constructively though, in which case it's definitely not a diagonal argument. For example, here is a proof that |N|&lt;|R|: Lebesgue measure exists, and assigns measure 1 to [0,1] and measure 0 to every countable set, therefore [0,1] is not countable. &gt;I'm not sure how to visualise it, as it doesn't really make much sense to me having a function that is dependent on itself? The function isn't dependent on itself, the *rate of growth* of the function is dependent on the *current value* of the function. For a concrete example, imagine a bank account with a 5% interest rate. If you have $100 in the account, you get $5 interest per year. If you have $1000, you get $50. The amount of interest depends on the current balance. For a second example, think of population growth. The rate of growth depends on the total population size: if you have twice as many couples, you get twice as many babies. It turns out that *lots* of functions have rates that depend on the value of the function in some way. This is the field of differential equations. The kind of equation you're studying, where the rate is *proportional to* the value of the function rather than some other kind of dependence, is called "exponential growth".

The denominator of a fraction is a *noun*. You can't count to fourth. Try it: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven... you never hit "fourth". That's because "fourth" is a noun, not a number. When you have "three fourths", that's like having three nickels, or three bananas, or three *of* something. The number "3/4" is not somehow a mishmash of the numbers 3 and 4; it's 3 of a thing called fourths. If these kids have a bit of familiarity with money, talk about coins. What's 2 pennies plus 3 dimes? 32 cents. Wait, where did "cents" come from? Why can't you just add 2+3? You can't do that because it *doesn't even make sense* to add things up unless they're the same kind of thing. Likewise, if you want to add "2 thirds plus 3 quarters", it doesn't even make sense to add 2+3 in the numerator or 3+4 in the denominator, for the same reason it doesn't make sense to add penny plus dime to get pennydime. It's two of something, plus three of something else. If you want to figure out how much that is total, you need to describe them both with the same noun. For money, you use cents: 2 pennies is 2 cents, 3 dimes is 30 cents. For fractions, you use a common denominator: 2 thirds is 6 twelfths, 3 quarters is 9 twelfths.

Many games use some kind of rating system, with ELO being the most famous. There are variants for team matchmaking as well. A simple version would be to just average the ratings of everyone to get the team rating. In ratings systems, you get a certain number of points for winning or losing. The higher your rating (relative to your opponent), the fewer points you get and the more you lose. If your rating is "correct", your wins and losses will balance out over time so that your rating stays the same. This means your rating is implicitly an estimate of your chance to win. For example, if playing against someone at a certain rating earns you +20 points for a win and -10 for a loss, then you'll break even if you win 1/3 of your games and lose 2/3 of your games.

Does that mean she drank *all of* the milk and coffee? Certainly not! You shouldn't add together the two fractions this way. For a less algebraic approach, notice that if everyone drank the same proportion of milk and coffee, they'd all get equal fractions of each: 1/3 if there are 3 people, 1/5 if there are 5, etc. But if you decide to drink more milk and less coffee, you'll get more than your share of the milk, and less than your share of the coffee. Since the two fractions for Angela are 1/4 and 1/6, that 1/4 must be more than her share of milk - ie, there are more than 4 people - and that 1/6 must be less than her share of coffee - ie, there are less than 6 people.
