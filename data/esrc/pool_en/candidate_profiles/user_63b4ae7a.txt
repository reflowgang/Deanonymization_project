Network security involves the same kinds of issues no matter what OS you're using. Any OS can be hardened, and any OS can be made vulnerable. If you don't respond to vulnerabilities and rely solely on a vendor solutions, you'll have problems. An old OS like WinXP has had time to be attacked in a zillion ways, meaning that most of its inherent vulnerabilities have been long known, with multiple tools widely available to exploit them. XP is a very stable OS, and its age confers some limitations; it can't do all the things that 8.1 or 10 can do, but it's significantly less complex. The problem wasn't Windows XP, it was poorly administered XP. By using rolling backups and tripwire/OSSEC-type tools-- or even simply storing hashes of all system, executable, and library files--you can indeed determine when an intrusion happened. Again, this requires diligent, knowledgable admins working to a clear plan. If your recovery plan consists of simply rolling a server back to some "known-good" state, you aren't doing things properly. There should be detailed records kept, allowing IT to rebuild a contaminated server with clean OS, applications, and data. Data backup should always be done as frequently and as completely as is possible. Arguing that designing and enacting a comprehensive security model is too hard is admitting defeat at the outset. If you take *any* OS, remove all unnecessary services, close all unnecessary ports, and do things like scrubbing a system of identifiers, you've got the beginnings of a secure system. The next steps are articulating and enforcing security on the human side of things--bad/reused passwords, no 2FA, no training for social engineering stuff... that'll sink any ship. The biggest &amp; most successful systems penetrations known (ie, the government actor stuff; 8200, SSF, etc.) all relied heavily on human fallibility. You can secure (or expose) any OS. I started doing it professionally just prior to the release of SunOS 4.1.3. I stared to get frustrated by the volume of attacks on pur Sun machines. I noticed that the Stratus, running FTX, a high-availability machine made for the financial and insurance industries, would get poked at, and then left alone. There were no published FTX vulnerabilities at that time. I went through every damned Sun and SunOS identifiers on our Sparc 20 servers and made them look like Stratus/FTX machines. The volume of attacks decreased, eventually slowing to a trickle. The city should have been using better security procedure. I don't know if I would have moved them away from XP/Server or not... probably yes, , but it is possible to lock down an XP box and use the same kinds of modern access controls and intrusion detection tools available to enterprise servers. Obviously, they screwed up.

It isn't the circuit, it's *phase*. Most residential power (in the US) comes into the breaker box with a neutral leg, and two hot legs. (*hot* means it's carrying current.) Each of these legs carries alternating current (cycling from positive, to zero, and then negative at 60 cycles per second, called "60 Hertz,") out of phase with each other, meaning when one is at full positive current, the other is at full negative current. They are 180 degrees out of phase with one another. Between either of the two hot legs and neutral, you get 110 volts. Between the two hot legs, you get 220v. Some circuits will be on one phase, while others are on the other. Many washers and dryers use 220 volts, meaning they span two phases. Powerline adapters communicate at radio frequencies. You can buy a bridge that'll pass those frequencies from phase to phase, also called a phase coupler. RF communication may be interfered with by electrically "noisy" things like motors (fans, dryers, washing machines, air conditioners, refrigerators, and stuff like old-school fluorescent light ballasts.
