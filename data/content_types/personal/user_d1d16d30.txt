I worked in physical therapy for a while. BS, physiology (emphasis exercise science) and CSCS.  When someone says 'steroids', they could mean a number of things. Common parlance means *anabolic* steroids. Anabolic means muscle-building. Those are the steroids that bike racers pop hot for. They are illegal and banned for many, many reasons. Many times, dopers are taking 100-1000x a clinical dose of these drugs, so their side effects are massive. In the worst case scenario, tendons become so frail that they rip. In fact, that's a dead giveaway in bodybuilding: if someone has tendon rupture, they're juicing. There are many other side effects, and you've heard of them: hairy women, boobs on men, small testicles, and those are the *pleasant* ones, because they won't kill you. Guaranteed cancer is pretty much the endgame for those drugs.  Now, you're also talking about steroids for anti-inflammatory? Well, same thing as above - the steroids we use range from corticosteroids (cortisol being the one most common) to inhaled steroids (Symbacort/Formoterol, Advair, Flovent/Fluticasone). The short answer is that there are approximately *hella* side effects. Everything from mood swings to acne to bloating.  The other problem is that a lot of people don't respond well to corticosteroids. A certain percentage of people are allergic, and then a lot of issues aren't inflammation related. As a rough estimate, 30% of people who had a cortisone injection (meaning that they were good candidates: their injury looked like it would respond, it fit their treatment pattern, and the doc chose to administer it) did not respond.  Comparatively, NSAIDs are almost no-risk. The only side effect of ibuprofen is that a clinical dose for an extended period can cause stomach ulcers. They are relatively effective for 90% of people, and they're cheap. They're incredibly easy to administer. Cycling injuries tend to be severe and chronic, however. They build for a long time before a cyclist chooses to pay attention to them, so they'll take a long time to go away. To top it off, cyclists aren't going to stop cycling long enough for the injury to heal.  Frankly, we probably should be more ready to use cortisol in young, healthy cyclists. However, doctors that you and I have access to will be reluctant because it's a tradeoff. High level sports docs attached to sports teams will often be much more ready to throw cortisol at an athlete, since they understand longevity is not a concern but immediate performance is.  TL;DR: Tons of side effects. Docs want to try conservative first.

Wall of text warning. Let me start by saying that I did everything *wrong* and then figured out how to do it right.  **0) Find out what you want to study.** That sounds really easy. I promise it's not. I recommend STEM of some sort. Computer science is a really good idea. Business isn't a bad idea if you're not opposed to getting an MBA (expensive) and have tons of people and leadership skills. Those can be refined, but they can't be taught. If you're numerically and scientifically inclined, engineering is probably the best way to go. It will be the most miserable four years of your life, but the rest of your life will be a cakewalk if you do it right.  **1) Start out at a community college.** The first two years of most majors* will be pretty similar: basic English, basic history, philosophy, etc. They call these 'core' classes. My school also referred to freshman and sophomore classes (100 and 200 level) as 'lower level'. Most state schools will accept an associates' degree, which community colleges offer, as completing all of your core lower divisions. Watch out for science (eg, engineering). Sometimes you take classes in the first two years that also count for your major; you would have to still take those (eg, calc 3).  Another awesome thing is that my community college offers 'guaranteed transfer' classes. These are usually things like what I mentioned above: Bio 101, ENG 110, PHIL 110, etc. They are promised to transfer to the exact same course at a state university. So, Bio 111 at community college is worth Bio 1110 at flagship state school.  Let me belabor this point for a moment: One semester of tuition at my community college was $1400. One semester at my flagship state school was $5500. Frankly, I had a better experience at the community college because of how much smaller the classes were. Plus, since I was taking 200-level courses, the students were shockingly dedicated – my microbiology and pathophysiology courses were the most prepared students I ever studied with, and that includes master’s level pre-med students.  **1.5) Plan your degree.** My university offered something called a Degree Audit. I suggest you actually find a list of courses required for your major. For example, my university would break their requirements down into subsections and then you had to [get certain number of hours from each subsection](http://www.colorado.edu/artsandsciences/student-resources/core-curriculum/written-communication). As you saw from the link, I can get my Written Communication credits from any of those courses. Turns out, I needed to take IPHY 1950, Scientific Writing for my degree anyway. Therefore, if I'd taken WRTG 1100 Extended First-Year Writing and Rhetoric, I would still have to take IPHY 1950, regardless. Therefore, be smart about what classes you can take. If you spend an extra 6-8 hours a semester planning your courses, you can save yourself a semester or two (at $5000 a semester , that’s a pretty good hourly rate) and get a killer schedule (community college: All classes Tuesday / Thursday. It was a 10 hour day, but I had 5 days a week off).  **2) Apply for scholarships and FAFSA.** You must apply for FAFSA to receive federal loans. You’re likely to get screwed, since you said that your family is in the picture. FAFSA assumes everyone lives in a nice, Stepford-wives neighborhood and your family will contribute like 30% of their annual income to your schooling. Unfortunately, I’ve personally experienced that isn’t the case. So plan on receiving zero federal aid. You won’t be independent for FAFSA purposes until you’re 24, married, or a couple other hard-to-find exemptions.  The next best thing, of course, is to apply for scholarships. I am a white male from an upper middle class family, so I certainly had limited options. Still, I got two scholarships ($1,000 / yr for one paper, because no one else applied) and $750 / yr for books. Wasn’t a lot, and I eventually lost the big one because I’m an idiot, but hey, it saved me $3,000.  Scholarships don’t just come from your school – they can also come from your church and your community.  Note on student loans: they aren’t the end of the world. Subsidized student loans are at 3.2% in some cases. That’s a phenomenal rate. Other loans are 5.8%; that’s decent. But if you take out more than a specific number, which hopefully you won’t need to, they will be at 6.8% or higher. That’s not a great rate. If you can work and still take classes, it will minimize your cost of living.  **3) Work in a lab / gain experience.** Look on Craigslist / glassdoor in your field of choice. Search for 'entry level'. What do you find? Well, the salary sucks, and people STILL want you to have 1-3 years' experience in your field. Sure, you could BS it and say, 'Oh, I took Chem 111 and 112, so I have a year's experience in a lab!' No you don't. What they want is for you to have taken on projects. For example, I did three semesters of paid work in a BIO lab. I was getting paid $10 / hr, which was terrible for how complex the work was, but I learned a ton and was offered $15 to continue at that lab. Turns out I took a job for $11 / hr anyway, because I was trying to get into PT school.  Similarly, my school has a phenomenal aerospace program. Why? Well it's certainly not because the school teaches the kids anything that other schools can't. That'd be ridiculous. Instead, our engineering programs have REALLY strong ties to employers with great internships. The aerospace program basically has your last year or 3 semesters be interning at Ball Aerospace, Lockheed, or Raytheon. In fact, one of my friends just finished his master's with an internship at one of those companies. They hired him on at $80K at year. Not bad for a 24 year old.  **4) It isn't *all* about the money.** Sure, engineering sounds great, making $80K a year, and sure, an MBA sounds great to make $100K a year. But I know an MBA that worked 70 hours a week through his late 20s to get to making that in his mid-thirties. Fun fact, he still works about 55 hour weeks on average, and travels about two weeks a month. Is it worth it? I can't tell you that. A job that seems really rewarding is definitely worth a small paycut, but unfortunately job satisfaction doesn't pay the bills.  **5) Consider the military.** If you don’t know what you want to do… consider enlisting. I know it’s not an option for a lot of kids, but it really should be. My highschool didn’t ask whether I was going to college; they asked * where* I was going to college. So even though his salary sucks, he actually effectively makes the same amount as I do – only difference is, his stipend isn’t taxed! Plus, he’ll have the GI bill when he gets out. A college is a single school at a university, or could be standalone. For example, my university had the College of Engineering and the College of Arts &amp; Sciences. Used to work at a (CO) worker's comp office.  If you hurt yourself at work, you notify your employer in writing. Soon - there is a timeline. Since they're a big chain, they will require you to sit down with HR and fill out paperwork. This is how it gets covered. They will ask you things like what happened, what you hurt, and if anyone saw it.  From there, you will go to a (workman's comp) doctor's office. Their option of choice will be posted in your breakroom. Do NOT pay for it; tell them it's workman's comp and they will bill your insurance. This is kind of a 'general practitioner' of sorts; he will likely do x-rays if it's bad or send you to an orthopedist (specializes in muscle - skeleton injuries). Frankly, I don't think it is bad, if it doesn't continue to hurt. You cannot be fired or punished for filing the claim, but you can be fired if you miss work for the claim in an at-will state like mine.  [More info](https://www.workplacefairness.org/file-workers-comp-kentucky)  As for the breaks - that's another issue. Read up on your laws. My state has a statute that says we get a break *when possible*, meaning servers rarely get breaks.  All of this information is legally mandated to be posted at your workplace, usually in the breakroom.
