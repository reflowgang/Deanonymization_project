The elements of the grounds are extracted roughly in this order:  * Caffeine * Flavor/Aromatic oils * Bittering agents  The first brew is going to get the caffeine and flavor oils and some of the bittering agents. Any subsequent brews are just going to extract more bittering agents, leaving you with flavorless, bitter coffee with very little caffeine content.  You can see this by making "cowboy coffee". Set a pot of water to boil. Dump in some coffee grounds, and taste the coffee after 5 seconds, 30 seconds, 1 minute, 5 minutes, 10 minutes. Your first taste will taste like dirty water. Later tastes will taste like how you think of as coffee - oily mouthfeel with some bitterness. The longer you let the coffee sit there boiling, the more it'll just taste of bitterness.

Games from your list I'm familiar with:  * **Dominion.** Rule wise, super easy. Draw 5 cards. On your turn, you got an action and a buy, plus any additional actions/buys provided by cards you play. Whoever has the most VP at the end wins. The devil is in learning to build your deck in a sensible way as you go along.  * **Suburbia**. Another rule simple game. On your turn, buy a tile, place a tile, and then adjust your income/reputation/population/assets as noted. Obtain your income, increase/decrease your population, turn is over. Devil is in figuring out how to balance your income/reputation to win.  * **Castles of Burgundy**. Figuring out how the tiles work together is the hardest part. But relatively simple. Roll two dice, use the dice to obtain tiles, place tiles or sell goods. The rule book is something of a mess - gets more detailed than it needs to be.  * **Alhambra**. Because of the choices, this one is a touch more difficult, but still learnable in 10 minutes. On your turn, draw money, buy a tile, place a tile from reserve, or swap two tiles' locations. The first two actions are easy to understand, but figuring out how to effectively use the reserve pile and tile swapping is somewhat maddening.  Of the above, I could teach you any in 10 minutes or less. You'll understand how the game works, but not how to play well. (Sort of like teaching someone how chess "works" is super easy - just explain the movement patterns of each piece and what check/checkmate means - but teaching someone how to be a good chess player is not easy at all.)
